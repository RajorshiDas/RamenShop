\section{Introduction}
A small ramen-ya sits on a quiet Japanese street. Its two-storey wooden shop has a dining counter with an open kitchen, two small tables, a cosy tatami room upstairs, a street with sidewalks, cherry and maple trees, and a lake with ducks behind it. This project builds that whole place as an interactive, real-time \textbf{OpenGL} program. You can fly around it with seven cameras, switch every lamp on and off, change between day and night, click the doors open, and play a small ramen-cooking game.

Everything is made in code. Every object is built from a few basic shapes, and every texture is painted by the program itself at start-up, so there are no model or image files to load. On top of the lighting and shading there are shadows, a refractive glass, a small ray tracer, and many moving things: a cyclist, a person, a cat, a rippling banner and a glass wind chime.

\subsection{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Model a believable scene (shop, upstairs room, street, nature) from basic shapes and 3D transformations.
\item Light it with ambient, diffuse and specular light from several lights that can be switched on and off.
\item Use procedural textures, and shade with both Gouraud and Phong shading.
\item Add shadows, glass refraction and real ray tracing.
\item Make the scene alive: animation, several cameras, mouse and keyboard interaction, and a small game.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Modelling from basic shapes}\label{sec:model}
Every object is assembled from a handful of primitives (Figure~\ref{fig:prim}): a cube, a cylinder and cone (one routine with a bottom and a top radius), a sphere, a torus, planes and wedges. Boxes of other sizes are cubes with non-uniform scale and ellipsoids are scaled spheres. Three more constructions make the special shapes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lathe.} A profile $(r,y)$ is turned about the $y$ axis: $(r\cos\theta,\ y,\ r\sin\theta)$. Lamp shades, bowls, bottles and the wind-chime bell are made this way.
\item \textbf{Grid surface.} The nobori banner is a $12\times28$ grid of quads moved by a travelling wave, calm at the pole and strong at the free edge.
\item \textbf{Recursive trees.} Each branch grows shorter, thinner child branches; leaves or flowers sit on the tips.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{3D transformations}\label{sec:xf}
All placement goes through one function that applies the standard transform to the modelview matrix:
\begin{equation}
M = T(\mathbf p)\,R_x(\alpha)\,R_y(\beta)\,R_z(\gamma)\,S(s_x,s_y,s_z),
\end{equation}
so a part is scaled first, then rotated, then moved. Nesting parts with the matrix stack gives hierarchical models: a child inherits everything its parent does. Table~\ref{tab:xf} lists where each transformation is used and Figure~\ref{fig:hier} shows the joint hierarchies.

\midrule

For the cyclist the knee is found from the hip $\mathbf h$, the pedal $\mathbf f$ and the bone lengths $L_1,L_2$ with the law of cosines. With $d=|\mathbf f-\mathbf h|$ (at most $L_1+L_2$),
\begin{equation}
a=\frac{L_1^2-L_2^2+d^2}{2d},\qquad h=\sqrt{L_1^2-a^2},\qquad \mathbf k=\mathbf h+a\,\hat{\mathbf u}+h\,\hat{\mathbf u}^{\perp},
\end{equation}
so the knee always bends forward and the shin follows the pedal exactly. A wheel of radius $r$ that has rolled a distance $d$ has turned by $\theta=d/r$, and the cyclist moves along the street by $x(t)=x_0+(v\,t \bmod L)$.

\subsection{Textures and texture mapping}\label{sec:tex}
There are 13 procedural textures, each painted by a function when the program starts (Table~\ref{tab:tex}). Most are made from \emph{value noise} summed over several octaves (fractal Brownian motion) and wrapped so that they tile. The noise is a smoothly interpolated random lattice and the fractal sum adds finer octaves at half the strength:
\begin{equation}

\mathrm{fBm}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}2^{-(k+1)}\,n(2^kx,\,2^ky).
\end{equation}
They are used with the lighting (the light darkens and tints them), with mipmaps and anisotropic filtering so that they do not shimmer, and with planar mapping: the texture coordinates of a face are its size divided by a scale, so a plank is always the same width whatever the box.

\subsection{Illumination: ambient, diffuse and specular light}\label{sec:light}
OpenGL gives 8 lights, and each light $i$ has its own ambient, diffuse and specular colour. The colour of a surface point with unit normal $\mathbf N$, light direction $\mathbf L$ and half vector $\mathbf H=\widehat{\mathbf L+\mathbf V}$ is
\begin{equation}
I=\mathbf e+\sum_i a_i\Big[\mathbf c_a\mathbf l_{a,i}+\mathbf c_d\mathbf l_{d,i}\max(\mathbf N\!\cdot\!\mathbf L_i,0)+\mathbf c_s\mathbf l_{s,i}\max(\mathbf N\!\cdot\!\mathbf H_i,0)^{n}\Big],\qquad a_i=\frac{\sigma_i}{k_c+k_l d+k_q d^2},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf e$ is the emission of the material (lantern paper, bulbs), $\mathbf c_{a,d,s}$ are the material colours, $n$ is the shininess and $a_i$ the attenuation. For a spot light $\sigma_i=(\cos\alpha)^e$ inside the cone (cut-off $28^\circ$, $e=35$) and $0$ outside. The global ambient light is zero, so a lamp that is off really lights nothing.

Only 8 lights exist, but the shop needs more, so the four lights that normally serve the outside (entrance lanterns, street lamps, sign) are re-assigned to interior roles while the inside is drawn (Table~\ref{tab:lights}). Keys \code{1}--\code{7} switch each fixture, \code{0} the sun or moon, and \code{-}, \code{=}, \code{\textbackslash} remove just the ambient, diffuse or specular term from every light.

\subsection{Glossy surfaces and the specular term}
The specular term makes a surface look glossy. For a light of specular colour $\mathbf l_s$ and a material of specular colour $\mathbf c_s$ and shininess $n$ it adds
\begin{equation}
I_s=\mathbf c_s\,\mathbf l_s\,(\mathbf N\!\cdot\!\mathbf H)^{n}\,a,
\end{equation}
which is brightest when the half vector lines up with the normal, that is when the eye sees the mirror image of the lamp. The exponent $n$ sets how narrow the highlight is (Figure~\ref{fig:lobe}): a small $n$ gives a broad weak sheen (plaster, cloth), a large $n$ a small sharp spot (glass, glaze). Table~\ref{tab:spec} lists the materials. For ordinary materials the specular colour is white; for metals it is tinted by the surface colour, which is why brass looks gold and steel looks grey. Roughness $r$ maps to shininess by $n=4+124(1-r)^2$.

\subsection{Gouraud and Phong shading}\label{sec:shade}
With key \code{G} the whole scene switches between two shading models.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gouraud shading} (fixed function): the lighting equation is evaluated at each vertex and the colour is interpolated across the polygon, $C=\lambda_1C_1+\lambda_2C_2+\lambda_3C_3$. It is fast, but a highlight that falls between vertices disappears.
\item \textbf{Phong shading} (my GLSL 1.20 shader): the normal is interpolated, $\mathbf N=\widehat{\lambda_1\mathbf N_1+\lambda_2\mathbf N_2+\lambda_3\mathbf N_3}$, and the same equation is evaluated for every pixel, for all 8 lights, with spot cones, attenuation, fog and a per-light on/off mask.
\end{itemize}
Flat shading (one colour per polygon) was not implemented. Fog uses $f=e^{-(\rho z)^2}$. Table~\ref{tab:shade} compares the two models and Figure~\ref{fig:shade} shows the difference.

\subsection{Shadows}\label{sec:shadow}
Three kinds of shadow are used (Figure~\ref{fig:shadow}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Outdoor sun shadows.} The whole outdoor scene is drawn a second time, flattened onto the ground along the sun direction $\mathbf s$ with a shear matrix that sends every point to
\begin{equation}
x'=x-\frac{s_x}{s_y}\,y,\qquad y'=h,\qquad z'=z-\frac{s_z}{s_y}\,y.
\end{equation}
The flattened drawing goes only into the stencil buffer, then one soft dark layer is blended over the marked pixels, so overlapping branches never darken twice. At night only a few simple fixed soft shadows are drawn.
\item \textbf{Interior shadows.} The furniture is projected onto the tiled floor from the pendant and table lamps with the point-light version of the same matrix.
\item \textbf{Soft shadows (key \code{Y}).} The counter, stools, tables, chairs and bowls are drawn from the pendant light into a $512\times512$ depth texture. In the shader every pixel is compared with the map $3\times3$ times and the results are averaged, $s=\frac19\sum_k[z_L\le z_{map,k}+\epsilon]$ (percentage-closer filtering), so the edge is soft.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ray tracing}\label{sec:rt}
Key \code{R} adds a real ray tracer that runs on the CPU for eight featured objects (Table~\ref{tab:rtobj}). The rest of the scene is still drawn by OpenGL; the ray tracer only decides the pixels of these objects (Figure~\ref{fig:rtflow2}).
\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.4cm}p{1.2cm}p{4.4cm}p{6.7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Object} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Shapes} & \textbf{What the rays do}\\
\midrule
Steel stock pots & 2 & cone, top disk & reflect the kitchen, lamps and counter\\
Ramen bowls & 4 & foot ring, body, soup disk & glaze reflects the lamps; soup reflects weakly\\
Glass ball & 1 & sphere, radius 0.075 & reflects and refracts (Snell, $n=1.5$)\\
Mirror & 1 & slab $0.8\times1.3$ in a frame & near-perfect reflection of the upstairs room\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The ray-traced objects. All other shapes (floors, walls, counter, tables, lamps) exist only so that rays have something to hit.}\label{tab:rtobj}
\end{table}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scene.} The shop is rebuilt for the rays from the same positions and sizes as the OpenGL models, with the same textures and the live OpenGL lights. Lamps are light-emitting shapes, so they show in reflections.
\item \textbf{Intersection.} Exact formulas: slab test for boxes, quadratic equations for spheres and cones, plane test for disks. A \textbf{bounding volume hierarchy} (BVH) groups the small shapes in boxes, so a ray skips everything inside a box it misses.
\item \textbf{Shading.} Surfaces are lit with the same Blinn--Phong model. Metal, glaze, soup and the mirror send a reflection ray, up to three bounces; the glass ball bends rays with Snell's law:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r=\mathbf d-2(\mathbf d\!\cdot\!\mathbf n)\mathbf n,\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2,\qquad F=F_0+(1-F_0)(1-\cos\theta)^5 .
\end{equation}
Reflection and refraction are weighted by the Fresnel term $F$; if the refracted ray does not exist the ray is totally reflected.
\item \textbf{Speed.} Only the screen rectangles of these objects are traced, the rows are shared by up to 8 CPU threads, and a ray budget keeps the pass near 25 ms by tracing one ray per $2\times2$ block when a large area is in view. In a close view (about 7,000 eye rays) it takes about \textbf{13 ms in a Release build} and 80 ms in Debug.
\item \textbf{Compositing.} The result is drawn over the OpenGL frame only where the depth buffer shows the same surface, so a nearer object still hides a pot or the ball.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Wind chime physics.} The bell (angle $\theta_1$, length 0.12 m) and the clapper with its paper strip ($\theta_2$, length 0.20 m) are pendulums driven by a gusty wind $w(t)$ and integrated with 8 small steps per frame:
\begin{equation}
\ddot\theta_i=-\frac{g}{L_i}\sin\theta_i-c_i\dot\theta_i+k_i\,w(t),\qquad i=1,2.
\end{equation}
The paper strip catches much more wind than the glass ($k_2=22$ against $k_1=3$), so the clapper swings further; when the relative angle reaches $0.42$ rad it hits the glass, bounces back and a star-shaped glint flashes on the rim.

\subsection{Cameras, interaction and controls}\label{sec:cam}
The world uses a perspective projection ($45^\circ$, $62^\circ$ in game mode); for field of view $\phi$, aspect ratio $a$ and clip planes $n,f$ it is
\begin{equation}
P=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{a\tan(\phi/2)}&0&0&0\\0&\frac{1}{\tan(\phi/2)}&0&0\\0&0&\frac{f+n}{n-f}&\frac{2fn}{n-f}\\0&0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so after the division by the depth, far objects look smaller. The game screens use an orthographic projection (no division by depth) so that the 2D overlay keeps its pixel size. A click is turned into a world ray and intersected with the plane of each door; the hit point is tested against the door's rectangle. Keys select an object (Tab) and move, rotate and scale it. \subsection{The ramen cooking game}\label{sec:game}
The game is a thin layer on top of the shop: a state machine, four stations, a few animated 3D objects and a 2D HUD. It is opt-in: the program starts in explore mode and \code{Enter} opens the title screen. A customer walks in and sits down and an order appears; the player takes raw noodles to the noodle pot, takes a bowl, scoops the noodles out when they look perfectly boiled, ladles broth, adds the toppings and serves the bowl (Figure~\ref{fig:game}). The noodle colour changes smoothly through five states: raw (under 3 s), undercooked, perfect (6--9 s), overcooked and ruined (over 12 s).

\begin{equation}

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{What worked.} Building every object from a few primitives kept the code small and the scene consistent, and the per-light switches made the lighting model easy to inspect. The stencil trick gives clean, non-overlapping tree shadows cheaply, and the mix of rasterisation with a small ray tracer shows real reflection and refraction without tracing the whole scene.

\textbf{Problems and how they were solved.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Only 8 lights.} The outdoor lights are re-assigned to interior fixtures while the inside is drawn, and a shader mask keeps outside lights from lighting the interior through the walls.
\item \emph{Lights switched off but the room still lit.} A global ambient and glowing materials caused it; the global ambient is now zero and glowing surfaces fade with their lamp.
\item \emph{Broken, streaky shadows.} The shadow planes lay a few millimetres above the ground and fought with it (z-fighting); depth testing is off for that pass.
\item \emph{Blocky ray-traced objects up close.} The ray budget keeps the cost bounded, so a large object gets one ray per several pixels; the drinking glasses and the jug, which looked worst, are drawn by OpenGL, and the table tumbler keeps its screen-space refraction.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Limitations.} Flat shading is not implemented; refraction of the tumbler is a screen-space approximation; ray tracing covers only eight interior objects and uses no shadow rays; the outdoor night shadows are simple fixed shapes; there is no sound. \textbf{Possible improvements:} cube-map shadow maps for each lamp, a shadow map for the sun, ray tracing on the GPU with a GPU BVH, and customers that walk into the shop by themselves.

% =====================================================================
%                          6 CONCLUSION
% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\textbf{The Ramen Shop} is a complete, explorable 3D scene written from scratch with OpenGL. It implements the proposed features (transformations, hierarchical models, a perspective camera, ambient, diffuse and specular light, Gouraud and Phong shading, a skybox and procedural textures; the orthographic view is used for the game screens and flat shading was not implemented) and adds three kinds of shadow, screen-space glass refraction, real ray tracing with a BVH, animated characters, a physically simulated wind chime and a playable cooking game. All of it runs interactively from a single Visual Studio project with no external assets.
